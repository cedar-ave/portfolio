Here are five hard-won considerations to take into account when choosing an open-source static-site generator.

{/* truncate */}

## How long has the tool been around?

I learned this question from a tech writer I met at a [Write the Docs](https://www.writethedocs.org) conference. The conference was abuzz with a new open-source static-site generator that had just launched. I asked him what he thought about it. He said, “It may sound cool, but before jumping into it you need to know a couple things." Then he shared these questions:

* How long has it been around?
* Will it be maintained over time?

"Don't get locked into a flash-in-the-pan," he said. He was right. After the conference, I never heard the tool mentioned again. (Kudos to those who built the tool, though: for being talented enough to build something new and brave enough to share it.)

## Who owns it?
If a large, established company has built their entire docs ecosystem on an open-source tool (like Docusaurus with Meta) there's a strong motivation there to maintain and enhance it.

## How much is it used?
The more companies and people who use it, the more you're going to see templates, add-ons, extensions, etc., shared. The number of stars in a tool's Github repo can be one clue.

## How often is it updated?
A tool that's infrequently updated is probably less likely to support new requirements that emerge over time. A Github repo's code frequency graph can show you if contributions are waning.

## What are your content requirements?
It doesn't matter much for a simple portfolio site like mine, but if you're choosing a tool to support an entire body of content, you may or may not need features like:

* Content reuse  
* Ability to integrate with an existing app  
* Whether you can build an authoring interface around it for non-technical contributors  
* The ability to (easily) add custom features that may not be offered by the tool (like paragraph anchors, feedback forms at the bottom of articles, integrated chatbots, etc.)

## Who can help you when things break?
If you're not an engineer, this is huge. Seasoned tech writers who've built sites know it's not a good idea to implement any tool or technology that often requires asking an engineer to help you solve a problem (they have their own stuff to do). But once in a blue moon, you may run into an issue so far above your head that you do need to beg for engineering help. That's why in one role, I chose a tool that was built in the same programming language the engineering team used. That put as little friction as possible around diagnosis and resolution.
